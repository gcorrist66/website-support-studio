# Naming & Positioning — From "Revenue Leak Audit" to Owner Language

**Status:** Positioning and packaging only. The audit framework, deliverables,
scoring rubric, pricing, ladder economics, and customer journey defined in
`REVENUE_LEAK_AUDIT_OFFER_DESIGN.md` and `REVENUE_LEAK_AUDIT_LANDING_PAGE_DESIGN.md`
are **unchanged**. This document renames and re-languages what they're called
to a customer. Nothing deployed, pushed, or modified in production.

**Owner:** Corriston Consulting, LLC
**Date:** June 12, 2026

---

## 1. Executive Summary

"Revenue Leak Audit" fails the only test that matters: the founder heard it and
said "WTF is that?" — and he *sells* it. The name is built from three words an
owner never uses ("revenue," "leak"-as-metaphor, "audit") and the worst of them
is "audit," which means the IRS, or the free PDF every agency spams them with.

The fix is hiding in plain sight: **the trades already sell this exact product
to their own customers.** An HVAC company charges a diagnostic fee. A plumber
charges a service call to find the leak before quoting the fix. A roofer does
an inspection before talking price. They defend that fee to homeowners every
single day. So we sell them the same thing, in their own format:

> **The Missed Jobs Report** — a 37-point inspection of everything between
> your phone ringing and a job getting booked.

"Missed jobs" is the outcome they lose sleep over, in two words they already
say. "Report" is a thing you hold. "Inspection" is *their* word for paid
diagnosis. The sales narrative becomes one sentence any owner repeats
correctly to a spouse or business partner — which is the real test of a name.

Internally, nothing changes: same framework, same rubric, same $750, same
ladder. `RLA` stays the internal/file name. Only the customer-facing skin
changes.

## 2. Kill Consultant Language (the brutal review)

Test: would a roofing, HVAC, or plumbing owner — phone buzzing, in a truck,
between estimates — instantly understand it and want it?

### "Revenue Leak Audit" — FAIL
- **"Revenue"** — P&L vocabulary. Owners say *jobs, work, money, booked*.
  "Revenue" signals "consultant who reads dashboards," which is exactly the
  vendor class they distrust.
- **"Leak"** — the one defensible word, and still a problem: for a plumber
  it collides with their literal job ("you audit… pipe leaks?"), and as a
  metaphor it requires a beat of interpretation. Anything that requires a
  beat loses the truck test.
- **"Audit"** — the killer. An audit is something done *to* you (IRS, OSHA,
  workers' comp). And "free marketing audit" is the most spammed phrase in
  their inbox; pricing ours at $750 doesn't elevate it, it confuses it.
- Combined: three abstractions an owner has to decode. Gary's reaction is the
  market's reaction.

### "Revenue Leak Assessment" — WORSE
"Assessment" is HR and insurance language. Even less concrete than audit, with
a whiff of paperwork and committees. Dead on arrival.

### "Revenue Leak Diagnostic" — WRONG WORD ORDER, RIGHT INSTINCT
"Diagnostic" is the first word in this family the trades actually use — HVAC
techs charge diagnostic fees every day. But "Revenue Leak Diagnostic" still
leads with two consultant words and buries the familiar one. The insight to
keep: **don't borrow consulting formats; borrow THEIR formats** — the
diagnostic, the service call, the multi-point inspection, the checkup. That
reframe does more work than any individual word swap.

**Verdict:** kill all three as customer-facing names. Keep "Revenue Leak
Audit" only as the internal product code (existing docs, scoring workbook).

## 5. Ten Positioning Statements, Ranked

1. **"How many jobs did you miss last month? We'll show you — with proof."**
   The product in eleven words: the question they can't answer + the promise
   of evidence. Page hero material.
2. **"You inspect the roof before you quote it. We inspect your lead flow
   before you spend another marketing dollar."** Their own logic, turned
   around. Best for outreach to the 72 (feels written for them, swap the
   trade per vertical).
3. **"We call your business like a customer. What happens next tells you
   everything."** Concrete, slightly menacing, instantly imaginable. Best
   cold-ad line.
4. **"Stop buying more leads until you stop losing the ones you have."**
   The strategic reframe of the whole offer; disqualifies lead-gen vendors in
   one sentence.
5. **"Your next $10,000 job may have already called. Did anyone answer?"**
   Loss aversion with a number. Strong, but hypothetical-sounding if overused.
6. **"Marketing reports show clicks. Ours shows missed jobs."** Positions
   against every report they currently get. Great for the ads-running segment.
7. **"We find the jobs you're losing between the phone ringing and the work
   getting booked."** The honest one-line scope statement. Subheadline duty.
8. **"Every missed call is a job your competitor booked."** True and
   visceral; as a lead line it's fear-first, better as supporting copy.
9. **"Find out where the money's leaking — before you pour more in."**
   Solid bucket metaphor; less specific than 1–7.
10. **"You're already paying for the phone to ring. We make sure you know
    what happens when it does."** Accurate but flat; the setup without the
    punch.

**Use:** #1 anchors the page, #7 sits under it, #2 opens warm outreach,
#3 and #6 are ad variants, #4 is the sales-call thesis.

## 6. Five Offer Angles

| Angle | The pitch in one line | Who it hits hardest | Verdict |
|-------|----------------------|--------------------|---------|
| **Missed jobs** | "Jobs you never knew you lost — found, priced, and listed." | Every owner; a "job" is concrete (a truck roll, a name, a dollar figure) | **PRIMARY.** A missed job has a face and a price; "revenue" has neither. Everything else supports this. |
| **Phone ringing** | "We call your business like a customer and show you what happens." | Owner-operators who believe "we answer every call" | **DEMONSTRATION angle.** The most *believable* proof we offer — leads ads and cold outreach because it's testable in the reader's head, then hands off to missed-jobs. |
| **Marketing waste** | "Find out which of your marketing dollars buy jobs — and which buy nothing." | The segment already spending $1K+/mo on ads/SEO | **SEGMENT angle.** Strongest for the ads-running subset (likely most of the 72); slightly narrows the offer if led with globally. |
| **Money** | "We show you where money is leaking out of your business every month." | Numbers-minded owners | **SUPPORT.** True but abstract; becomes powerful only after the leak map prices each leak — use in the report, not the headline. |
| **Lead quality** | "Stop paying for tire-kickers and out-of-area calls." | Businesses drowning in junk leads from aggregators | **NICHE.** Real pain, narrowest reach; one section of the page, one email variant — not a lead angle. |

**Resonance call:** lead with **missed jobs**, open cold conversations with
**phone ringing**, target the ads-spenders with **marketing waste**. Money and
lead-quality angles live inside the report and page sections, not in headlines.

## 7. The Sales Narrative (final)

What Gary says — on a call, at a counter, in a warm email. Sixty seconds,
no consultant words, repeatable by the listener afterward:

> **"You know how you charge a diagnostic fee to find out what's wrong with an
> AC unit before you fix anything? I do the same thing for the part of your
> business that books jobs.**
>
> **I call your company like a customer — daytime and after hours. I fill out
> your forms and time how long it takes anyone to reply. I check whether the
> towns you actually serve can find you on Google. And I look at what happens
> to leads after they come in — who answers, how fast, and who follows up on
> the quotes that go quiet.**
>
> **Then I hand you a report that shows exactly where jobs are slipping away,
> what each leak costs you per month, and what to fix first — with proof, not
> opinions. It's $750, takes two weeks, and needs about two hours of your
> time. And if your operation checks out clean, the report says so, and the
> honest answer is you don't need me."**

Three sentences shorter, for the truck test:

> "I test your business like a customer — calls, forms, Google, follow-up —
> and show you exactly where you're missing jobs and what each miss costs you.
> $750, two weeks, proof included. If nothing's wrong, the report says that too."

The structural trick: the first sentence is *their own business model handed
back to them*. Nobody who charges a diagnostic fee can argue with paying one.

## 9. Recommendation

**Rename to The Missed Jobs Report. Sell it as their own service call, turned
around.** Keep "Revenue Leak Audit" as internal code only. Then validate the
same way the problem was found: say it to Gary cold — *"It's a Missed Jobs
Report — I test your calls and forms like a customer and show you which jobs
you're missing"* — and watch whether the reaction is "WTF is that?" or "how
much?" Run the same five-second test on 3–5 owners from the 72 list (or any
trades owner within reach) before printing anything. The bar: they can repeat
what it is, in their own words, after hearing it once.

Sequencing note for the existing launch plan (landing-page doc §15): nothing
re-orders. The sample scorecard gets built under the new name from the start —
cover says "The Missed Jobs Report," score page says "Your Booked-Job Score" —
so no asset is ever produced under the dead name.

One discipline going forward, worth writing on the wall: **if a word wouldn't
be said across a tailgate, it doesn't go on a customer surface.** "Attribution,"
"conversion," "diagnostic," "audit," and "revenue" all live in the workbook;
"jobs," "calls," "quotes," "proof," and "missed" live on the page.
